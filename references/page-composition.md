# Page composition and restraint

Use for whole-page finishing passes, new sections, or problems with widths, section rhythm, columns, heading wraps, and content density. Inspect the page at normal viewing size as well as section by section. An isolated component can look correct while the overall page feels inconsistent.

## Preserve creative sections

Identify ordinary sections that establish the page's baseline and intentional focal sections that change its pace. A large editorial statement, full-bleed media sequence, oversized statistic, asymmetric collage, or animated type composition may deliberately use different sizes, widths, spacing, and proportions. Look for support in the reference, brief, or coherent composition; a difference alone proves neither intent nor a defect.

Keep those sections' defining relationships intact. Correct concrete issues such as clipping, broken responsive behavior, or accidental control inconsistencies locally. Do not force a creative section into the ordinary container, heading size, two-column balance, or hero line-count target just to reduce variation. If its intent remains uncertain, leave the expressive choice intact and explain the uncertainty.

Example: ordinary feature headings drift between several sizes, while an interlude uses a deliberately oversized statement. Normalize the feature headings to their shared role and preserve the interlude's scale. Inspect the transitions into and out of it for accidental spacing problems without removing the intentional change of pace.

## Common content edges

Identify the main content container, its maximum width, and responsive side gutters. Compare the logo/navigation content, hero, ordinary sections, and footer against that system. A conventional wide navbar is a useful alignment reference; distinguish its outer background or border from its inner content edges.

A floating compact navbar or side navigation does not determine every section's width. Use a shared container for the main content region instead. Preserve intentional narrow reading columns and full-bleed galleries or media. A full-width section background can still contain content aligned to the common grid.

Example: ordinary feature, testimonial, and CTA sections have unrelated side gutters with no compositional reason. Apply the established container to their content wrappers. Leave a deliberately edge-to-edge image alone. Check both left and right edges at the same viewport; do not fix only the left margin.

## Section rhythm and height

Compare padding above and below content, heading-to-body spacing, and spacing between sections of similar purpose. Trace adjoining padding and margins: a large gap may come from two sections both contributing space. Use shared spacing values or explicit compact/standard/spacious section treatments rather than independent guesses.

Treat unexplained differences between similar sections as candidates for correction. Keep hero, dense feature, testimonial, and footer proportions appropriate to their content. Consistent rhythm does not require identical total heights. Avoid fixed heights, excessive minimum heights, or viewport-height sections added merely to make section boundaries match. Let content grow with wrapping, zoom, and localization.

Example: two comparable feature sections use very different vertical padding despite similar content. Reuse the intended spacing treatment. A shorter logo strip remains shorter because its role differs.

## Two-column balance

Identify the intended relationship: shared top edge, vertical centering, aligned bottom actions, or matched visual panels. Check heading margins, text measure, media aspect ratio, internal padding, and content density before adjusting column heights.

For paired cards or panels intended to match, stretch the panel containers through grid or flex layout and align their internal actions consistently. Allow text to determine the necessary height. For copy beside an image, consider the visible media bounds and text group's placement; the text need not fill the image's entire height.

Example: paired feature cards should share outer top and bottom edges, but one CTA floats halfway up. Use a shared card layout that places actions at the bottom. Do not add blank lines or a hardcoded text height. For an intentionally short quote beside a portrait, preserve the asymmetry and align the group deliberately.

## Hero and heading composition

For a typical marketing hero on a wider screen, aim for a clear two- or three-line heading when its length calls for wrapping. One strong line is also valid. Five accidental lines, a stranded final word, or a heading that overwhelms the supporting content should trigger inspection. Treat line count as a composition target, not a universal constraint; narrow screens and different languages may need more lines.

Check the rendered font and weight, available column width, text measure, font size, and letter spacing. Fix an unnecessarily narrow wrapper before shrinking the title. Consider balanced wrapping where the project supports it. If copy editing is in scope, remove redundant phrases while retaining the proposition. Otherwise adjust layout or report the copy constraint.

Do not force a target line count using clipping, unreadably small text, excessive negative tracking, nonbreaking spaces, or screenshot-specific line breaks. Use intentional line breaks only when the reference or editorial direction requires them and verify the supported widths. Check the hero's heading, supporting copy, CTA, and media as one composition.

## Copy and decoration

For each eyebrow, paragraph, divider, badge, and emphasized phrase, ask what information or hierarchy it adds. Common candidates for removal are an eyebrow that repeats the heading, a paragraph that repeats the preceding sentence, a hairline between already-distinct blocks, and decorative italic words with no consistent purpose. These are contextual judgments; do not label every instance a defect.

When editing copy is in scope, state the main point once and shorten generic introductions or repeated claims. Preserve product facts, qualifications, meaningful links, functional labels, required disclosures, and the user's tone. Do not invent a stronger claim to replace a longer one. Preserve useful category labels, meaningful emphasis, and dividers that make dense content easier to scan.

Example: an eyebrow says “Our features,” the heading says “Explore our features,” and a paragraph says “Discover the features we offer.” Keep the useful heading or replace it with an accurate benefit drawn from the existing content; remove the redundant layers when authorized. Keep an eyebrow such as “For finance teams” if it adds audience context absent from the heading.

After removing content or decoration, inspect spacing and heading structure again. Remove leftover margins and wrappers when appropriate, without losing accessible names or semantic relationships. Report meaningful copy changes so the user can review the wording.
